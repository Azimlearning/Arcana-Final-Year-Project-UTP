## What it does
Meta-analysis of **47 effect sizes from 20 empirical studies (2018–2023), 4,563 students** across
experimental and control groups. Overall effect size **d = 0.83** — large — for PhET simulations
versus traditional approaches. Subgroup analysis shows differential effects by education level,
physics domain, performance outcome, and delivery mode, with the strongest effects at secondary
level and in abstract domains such as electricity and waves.

## Why it matters to Synapse
One number that does a lot of work: **d = 0.83**.

An Introduction that says "interactive simulations help students learn" is weak. One that says
"interactive simulations produce a large effect on physics learning outcomes (d = 0.83 across 4,563
students)" is a quantitative claim with a citation, and it sets up the contribution as addressing a
real and measured opportunity rather than a plausible-sounding one.

The subgroup finding matters too: the largest effects appear in **abstract domains** — electricity,
waves — precisely the concepts students cannot observe directly and where a static diagram is
weakest. That is a targeted argument for what Synapse should generate, and worth a sentence in the
Introduction or in the discussion of which questions benefit most from an artifact.

Being 2026 also helps the recency profile (≥40% of references from 2024–2026 per the reference
guide).

## The gap it leaves
Aggregates studies of the human-authored PhET catalogue. Says nothing about generated simulations,
and the effect size cannot be assumed to transfer — a generated artifact could plausibly do better
(tailored to the exact question) or much worse (subtly wrong, or poorly designed against CTML
principles). **Do not imply the d = 0.83 transfers to Synapse.** Cite it as evidence for the
category, then state plainly that whether generated artifacts retain the benefit is unmeasured and
is future work.

That distinction is exactly the kind of care reviewers reward and its absence is the kind of
overclaiming they punish.
